# BRC-242: Safety Labels for Connected Apps

Crumbs

## Abstract

This document specifies **safety labels**: attributed statements that a named party, a **labeler**, makes about a connected app, a site, an app key, a build or a catalog, and that a wallet shows to its user. [BRC-241](./0241.md) confines a catalog to saying what its curator chose and checked; labels let anybody warn the users of every wallet, including about applications no catalog lists, without creating a gatekeeper.

A label carries one value from a closed set. Four **threat** values interrupt the user once, at the moment they would pin, open or connect, naming who said it and when, with its evidence. Five **disclosure** values are shown without interrupting. No label says an application is safe, and none blocks. A labeler may add values in its own namespace, shown only in an application's details. Users subscribe to up to ten labelers, and a host may designate one baseline. Feeds are downloaded whole, so a labeler never learns what a user visits, and are published as BRC-241 chains, so a labeler cannot show different users different feeds and its warnings outlast its server.

## Motivation

### What a catalog cannot say

A catalog under [BRC-241](./0241.md) selects. Its curator can list an application at a stated review level and withdraw it with a reason. It cannot warn about an application it does not list, and it cannot warn the users of anybody else's catalog. The party best placed to recognise a drainer impersonating a wallet is often a security team that curates nothing, and the application most in need of a warning is one no reputable curator lists.

Nor is every connected app reached through a catalog. A user follows a link or scans a QR code, and their wallet's [BRC-116](../wallet/0116.md) prompt is the first and only thing between that site and their keys. A warning kept inside catalogs is missing at the moment the user decides whether to grant the site anything.

### What has been tried

**One central list.** MetaMask's phishing blocklist and WalletConnect's Verify API are valuable and are single sources: a user cannot add a second opinion or see who made a call and why.

**Reactive moderation by the host.** Telegram Mini Apps are not reviewed before launch, and campaigns of more than a hundred bots impersonating large brands ran on that basis.

**Composable moderation.** Bluesky lets anyone run a labeler, users subscribe to the ones they trust up to a cap, the client decides how labels are shown, and Bluesky's own moderation is a baseline, an arrangement that by early 2025 supported hundreds of third-party labelers.

**Curator-assigned disclosures.** F-Droid's anti-features, such as Ads and Tracking, are assigned by the packagers who review an app, not by its developer.

This document adopts Bluesky's composable arrangement and F-Droid's split between threats, which interrupt, and disclosures, which describe.

### Why there is no positive label

Every positive mark examined in preparing [BRC-241](./0241.md), such as Flathub's "verified", was read by users as a safety guarantee, whatever it attested. Review, which is what a positive mark honestly describes, belongs to catalogs, stated at a level with a date, so a label can only subtract from what a user believes about an application.

### Why labels never block

A blocking label would give each labeler a veto over every wallet that subscribes to it, and make a false label indistinguishable from censorship. A label protects by informing; the protection that depends on nobody's judgement is the wallet's own, since every consequential call still meets [BRC-116](../wallet/0116.md)'s prompt.

## Specification

The key words MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT, and MAY in this document are to be interpreted as described in RFC 2119. Terms defined in [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 1 are used as defined there.

**Versioning.** A consumer MUST reject a label feed whose major version it does not recognise, and MUST ignore fields and minor versions it does not recognise.

**Reserved words.** "Flagged" means that at least one subscribed labeler holds a current threat label on the subject, and is always followed by who. No surface may describe a subject as safe, clean or cleared because no label applies to it.

### 1. Terminology

- **Labeler**: a domain that publishes a label feed.
- **Label feed**: a document holding a labeler's current labels, published as a chain of kind `labels` under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6. Section 4.
- **Out of date**: the state of a feed no source has successfully refreshed for 7 days. Section 5.1.2.
- **Label**: one statement in a feed: a subject, a value, a date, and for threats, evidence. Section 4.2.
- **Subject**: what a label is about: a domain, a domain and its subdomains, an app key, a build, or a catalog. Section 4.3.
- **Threat label**: a label with one of the four values of section 3.1.
- **Disclosure label**: a label with one of the five values of section 3.2.
- **Subscription**: a user's choice to have a labeler's labels shown. Section 5.4.
- **Baseline labeler**: a labeler a host designates as always subscribed. Section 5.5.

### 2. Scope

2.1 This document defines one document a labeler publishes, the kind `labels` under which it is published on chain, and the behaviour of a host that consumes it.

2.2 It adds no [BRC-100](../wallet/0100.md) method and changes no permission rule. A label never grants, denies or alters a permission.

2.3 It does not define how a labeler reaches its conclusions, which is its policy's business under section 7.

### 3. Values

The nine core values of sections 3.1 and 3.2 are closed. A labeler MUST NOT publish any other value except a namespaced value under section 3.3, which is the only extension, and a host MUST ignore a label whose value is neither a core value nor a namespaced value it accepts under section 3.3.4. Section 8 says how the core set grows.

#### 3.1 Threat values

| Value | The labeler claims | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `malicious` | The subject deliberately harms its users: drains funds, steals keys or credentials, or requests grants in order to abuse them | MUST |
| `phishing` | The subject imitates a page or flow of another party to collect secrets, signatures or approvals | MUST |
| `impersonation` | The subject presents itself as another party, a brand, a wallet or an application, without being it | MUST |
| `compromised` | The subject is operated, or was legitimately operated, but is currently serving harmful code or is controlled by somebody other than its operator | MUST |

3.1.1 `compromised` exists separately because the right response differs. A user of a compromised application should stop using it until its operator recovers, and its operator is a victim. A labeler SHOULD retract `compromised` promptly once the operator has recovered, and SHOULD NOT convert it to `malicious` without new evidence.

3.1.2 `impersonation` is distinct from `phishing` because an impersonator need not collect anything to do harm. A fake wallet that takes deposits impersonates without phishing.

#### 3.2 Disclosure values

| Value | The labeler claims |
| --- | --- |
| `tracking` | The subject reports user activity to third parties for purposes other than providing the service |
| `ads` | The subject shows advertising |
| `custodial` | The subject holds users' funds or keys on its own systems, so that the user's wallet is not the only custodian |
| `no-source` | The subject's client source is not published |
| `unmaintained` | The subject appears abandoned: unchanged for a long period, unanswered, or failing |

3.2.1 Disclosures are neutral descriptions. Many good applications carry `custodial` or `no-source`, and a host MUST NOT present a disclosure label in the manner of a threat.

3.2.2 Labelers assign disclosures, because the disclosures users rely on are the ones the subject did not write.

#### 3.3 Namespaced values

3.3.1 A labeler MAY publish a value outside the nine, written `p <namespace> <value>`: the `p ` dispatch point of [BRC-98](../wallet/0098.md), a namespace that is the domain the feed's identifier names, a space, and a value of 1 to 32 lowercase ASCII letters, digits and hyphens. For example, a labeler at `rating.example` might publish `p rating.example mature`.

3.3.2 A labeler that uses a namespace MUST describe each of its values in its policy, and its feed SHOULD carry, for each value it uses, a `definitions` entry with the value, a name of up to 32 characters and a description of up to 140.

3.3.3 **A namespaced value never interrupts.** A host shows it only where section 6.2 shows disclosures, attributed to the labeler, with the labeler's name and description for it, and MUST NOT present it in the manner of a threat.

3.3.4 A host MAY ignore namespaced values entirely, and MUST ignore any whose namespace is not the domain the feed's identifier names.

### 4. The label feed

#### 4.1 Where feeds are declared

A labeler declares its feeds in a `metanet.labelers` array in the `/manifest.json` at its domain, alongside its [BRC-68](../peer-to-peer/0068.md) `metanet.trust` object.

```json
{
  "metanet": {
    "labelers": [
      {
        "id": "labels:security.example/threats",
        "start": "<txid>.<vout>",
        "url": "https://security.example/labels.json",
        "steps": "https://security.example/labels.beef"
      }
    ]
  }
}
```

The outpoint is shown as its form, not a value, because this document prints no transaction it cannot reproduce.

The fields (`id`, `start`, `url`, `steps`, `mirrors`), the two-way declaration when a user subscribes, and the rule against probing other paths are those of [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.1, with `labels:` in place of `catalog:`. The `start` is the outpoint of the feed's first root output, whose field 3 is `labels`.

4.1.1 A labeler MAY declare up to eight feeds, for example one per subject kind or per region. Each is its own chain, with its own start. They are one labeler for the purposes of subscription under section 5.4.

#### 4.2 The feed document

```json
{
  "metanetLabels": "1.0",
  "id": "labels:security.example/threats",
  "name": "Example Security Threat Feed",
  "description": "Phishing and drainer sites targeting BSV wallets",
  "policy": "https://security.example/label-policy",
  "labels": [
    {
      "subject": { "domain": "readlngs.example" },
      "value": "impersonation",
      "at": "2026-11-04",
      "ref": "https://security.example/cases/2026-0193",
      "note": "Imitates Readings at readings.example"
    },
    {
      "subject": { "key": "..." },
      "value": "malicious",
      "at": "2026-10-30",
      "ref": "https://security.example/cases/2026-0187"
    }
  ],
  "retracted": [
    { "subject": { "domain": "shop.example" }, "value": "compromised", "at": "2026-11-02", "ref": "https://security.example/cases/2026-0171" }
  ]
}
```

The app key in the second label is elided for the reason above. The chain of [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6 takes the place of a signature, counter and expiry; freshness is section 5.1's.

| Field | Requirement | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `metanetLabels` | MUST | `"1.0"` |
| `id` | MUST | `labels:<domain>/<local>`, the local part as in [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.2 |
| `name` | MUST | 1 to 48 characters |
| `description` | SHOULD | 1 to 140 characters, saying what the labeler covers |
| `policy` | MUST | HTTPS URL of the labeler's policy, section 7 |
| `mirrors` | MAY | Up to eight HTTPS URLs from which the document may also be fetched |
| `labels` | MUST | The labeler's current labels, possibly none |
| `retracted` | MAY | Labels withdrawn, section 4.4 |
| `definitions` | MAY | Descriptions of namespaced values, section 3.3.2 |
| `report` | SHOULD | HTTPS URL accepting reports, section 6.5 |

4.2.1 A feed is the labeler's complete current statement. A label present in one version and absent from the next has been retracted, whether or not the next version lists it under `retracted`.

4.2.2 A feed MUST NOT exceed 4 MiB or hold more than 20,000 labels and retractions together, and a host MUST reject one that does. A labeler with more to say publishes more than one feed under section 4.1.1, and its subscribers receive all of them under one subscription.

4.2.3 A labeler publishes a new version, as a version step under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6.3, whenever its labels change, and need not publish otherwise.

4.2.4 Each label has:

| Field | Requirement | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `subject` | MUST | Section 4.3 |
| `value` | MUST | Section 3 |
| `at` | MUST | The date the labeler applied it |
| `ref` | MUST for threats, SHOULD for disclosures | HTTPS URL of the labeler's public record: what was observed, when, and why it supports the value |
| `note` | MAY | Up to 140 characters, the labeler's own words |

4.2.5 **Evidence is required for threats.** A threat label without `ref` is not a valid label, and a host MUST ignore it. A claim shown to a user as a reason not to proceed must be one its labeler will justify in public.

#### 4.3 Subjects

A subject is exactly one of:

| Subject | Matches |
| --- | --- |
| `{"domain": d}` | The connected app or site whose normalised domain is `d` |
| `{"domainSuffix": d}` | `d` and every domain beneath it |
| `{"key": k}` | Every connected app whose app key, under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 3.5, is `k`, on whatever domain |
| `{"catalog": id}` | The catalog or label feed with that identifier |
| `{"build": h}` | Every application whose current client matches the BRC-244 aggregate hash `h`, on whatever domain |

4.3.1 **A key label follows the operator.** An operator labeled `malicious` who moves to a new domain under the same app key is labeled there too. An operator who moves under a new key is a new subject, which is why domain labels remain necessary.

4.3.2 **A suffix label covers campaigns.** Phishing operations register one domain and serve from many subdomains. A host MUST NOT apply a `domainSuffix` label whose `d` is, or is above, a public suffix in the Public Suffix List, because a label on a shared hosting suffix would label every tenant of it.

4.3.3 **A build label follows the code.** A malicious client copied to a new domain under a new key escapes both a domain label and a key label, and keeps its aggregate hash. A host that implements BRC-244 applies a build label only to an application whose client it has itself checked against a build manifest under that document, because a build label is a claim about exact bytes. A host that does not implement BRC-244 ignores build labels.

4.3.4 **A catalog label warns about a source.** A threat label on a catalog identifier is shown under section 6.2 when a user is about to add that catalog, and on every surface listing it. Where two catalogs share an identifier under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.2.6, a host applies the label to both and says so, since the identifier is all the label names.

4.3.5 **Labels and moves.** An app that moves under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 3.8 keeps its app key, so key labels follow it. Domain labels do not: a label on the old domain stays with the old domain, and a host shows it in the moved app's details, attributed, as a label on its former domain, and does not interrupt for it.

#### 4.4 Retraction

4.4.1 A labeler that withdraws a label SHOULD list it under `retracted`, with the date and a `ref`, for at least ninety days.

4.4.2 A host that showed a threat label to a user, and later finds it retracted, SHOULD tell that user once, on their next encounter with the subject, that the labeler withdrew it. A user who abandoned an application on a labeler's word is owed the correction as much as the warning.

### 5. Freshness, distribution and subscription

#### 5.1 Verification and freshness

5.1.1 A feed is a chain of kind `labels`, followed exactly as [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6.3 requires for catalogs, with documents checked under section 6.6.6 of that document and this document's size bound in place of the catalog's.

5.1.2 **Freshness.** A host SHOULD refresh each feed at least daily, and a refresh succeeds only as [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6.5 defines it, with answers from at least two sources under different registrable domains. A feed is **out of date** when the host's last successful refresh of it is more than 7 days old, by elapsed time on its own clock. Threats move faster than selections, so the period is shorter than a catalog's 31 days.

5.1.3 **A stale threat label is still shown.** A host holding a feed that is out of date MUST continue to show that feed's threat labels, marked with the date of the block holding the version they come from, and MUST NOT present the absence of newer information as a retraction. A stale warning costs a moment's thought; a stale silence costs funds.

5.1.4 **But not forever at full volume.** A threat label from a feed that has been out of date for more than 90 days, or whose chain ended more than 90 days ago, is shown only passively, under section 6.1.3, and never interrupts. A host that has heard nothing for that long cannot know the labeler still stands behind its claims.

5.1.5 A host MUST NOT present a feed's disclosure labels as current while the feed is out of date.

5.1.6 **Only mining gates a label.** A new threat label or a retraction takes effect when the version step carrying it is mined and a host has followed it, and waits on nothing else.

#### 5.2 Distribution

5.2.1 [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.7 applies to feeds as it does to catalogs: versions are accepted from any source, the labeler's domain is needed once, at subscription, and a domain that later declares a different start, or none, is handled under section 6.7.4 of that document. A labeler SHOULD publish every document under UHRP.

5.2.2 **A labeler's history is public.** Every version is a step on chain, so a labeler that labels a competitor and quietly retracts the label leaves a permanent record, and cannot show different users different feeds.

#### 5.3 Feeds are downloaded whole

5.3.1 A host MUST obtain labels by downloading feeds, and MUST NOT query a labeler about individual domains, keys or catalogs.

5.3.2 A per-subject query tells the labeler which sites the user visits, as they visit them. Google's Safe Browsing avoids the same disclosure, by downloading lists for matching on the device or by sending only hash prefixes through a privacy proxy. A feed downloaded on a schedule tells the labeler only that a host exists.

#### 5.4 Subscription

5.4.1 A user subscribes to a labeler by entering its domain, or a link `labels:<domain>/<local>?start=<txid>.<vout>` of the form of [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.2.1 with `labels:` in place of `catalog:`. The host compares the link's start with the one the labeler's manifest declares and completes the two-way declaration under sections 6.1.1 and 7.2.2 of that document, and MUST NOT subscribe to a feed whose declaration it has not completed, as section 7.2.4 of that document requires.

5.4.2 A subscription is to a labeler, and covers every feed it declares under section 4.1.1. A link to one feed subscribes to the labeler, and the host then confirms its other declared feeds. A feed is one feed wherever it was subscribed from if its start is the same, as [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.2.6 requires for catalogs, and a host never shows its labels twice.

5.4.3 Before subscribing, the host MUST show the labeler's domain, its `metanet.trust.name` attributed to it, its `description`, which values it uses and how many labels it holds of each, and a link to its policy. Where the labeler's `metanet.trust.name`, or a feed's `name`, has the same skeleton under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.5.1 as that of a labeler the user already subscribes to on another domain, the host MUST say so on that surface, naming both domains.

5.4.4 A host MUST allow at least five subscriptions and MUST NOT allow more than ten, counting a baseline labeler; between those bounds the cap is the host's choice. The cap counts labelers, not feeds. As on Bluesky, each subscription is a party whose claims will interrupt the user.

5.4.5 A host MUST let the user unsubscribe from any labeler other than a baseline labeler, and MUST NOT refuse a subscription on the ground of who the labeler is.

5.4.6 A host MAY suggest labelers, and MAY ship with labelers subscribed by default, each of which the user can remove.

#### 5.5 The baseline labeler

5.5.1 A host MAY designate at most one baseline labeler, whose threat labels are shown to every user of that host and whose subscription the user cannot remove.

5.5.2 A host that does MUST say, wherever subscriptions are managed, which labeler is the baseline and that it cannot be removed, and MUST let the user dismiss any individual baseline label under section 6.3.

5.5.3 The baseline exists because a new user subscribes to nothing, and is acceptable only because labels never block.

5.5.4 The baseline designation covers threat labels only. A host shows a baseline labeler's disclosure labels only if the user opts in to them, in the place where subscriptions are managed.

### 6. Presentation

#### 6.1 Threat labels

6.1.1 Where a subscribed labeler holds a current threat label on a subject, a host MUST show it before the user pins the app, opens it for the first time after the label appeared, connects to it under [BRC-116](../wallet/0116.md), or adds a labeled catalog.

6.1.2 It MUST show it on the same surface as any other warning the host owes at that moment, under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.9.3, and that surface MUST state for each label: the labeler's domain, the value in words, the date, and a way to open the `ref`.

6.1.3 It MUST also show a threat label wherever the subject is listed, without interrupting, so that a user browsing a catalog sees it before choosing.

6.1.4 It MUST let the user proceed. A threat label never blocks a pin, an open, a connection or a catalog.

6.1.5 Where several labelers label one subject, the host shows each, attributed. It MUST NOT combine them into a score, and MAY say how many subscribed labelers flagged it only alongside their names.

6.1.6 A catalog's review level, a home catalog's listing, and the user's own pin do not suppress a threat label.

6.1.7 In the [BRC-116](../wallet/0116.md) connection prompt, a threat label is shown for any site, listed in a catalog or not.

#### 6.2 Disclosure labels

6.2.1 A host MUST show current disclosure labels from subscribed labelers in the subject's detail view, attributed, in words, and MUST NOT interrupt the user for them.

6.2.2 A host MAY let the user hide subjects carrying particular disclosure values, and MUST NOT do so by default.

#### 6.3 Dismissal

6.3.1 A user MAY dismiss a threat label for a subject. A host MUST remember the dismissal for that labeler, subject and value, and interrupts the user again only if that labeler's label changes value or `ref`, or a different labeler applies a threat label. A label reissued with nothing changed but its `at` does not interrupt again, so a labeler cannot override a user's decision by re-dating its claim.

6.3.2 A host MUST NOT let a dismissal suppress the label anywhere it is shown passively, under section 6.1.3. Dismissal ends only the interruption.

#### 6.4 Words

6.4.1 A presentation names the labeler as the source of the claim: "security.example says this site impersonates another app", not "This site is an impersonator". The second puts the labeler's opinion in the host's voice.

6.4.2 A host MUST NOT state or imply that a subject with no labels is safe, checked or cleared.

#### 6.5 Reporting

6.5.1 A host SHOULD offer, wherever it shows an application or site, a control to report it.

6.5.2 The user chooses which labeler receives the report, from those they subscribe to whose feeds carry a `report` URL. A host MUST NOT send a report to a labeler the user did not choose, and MUST NOT send reports automatically.

6.5.3 A report is an HTTPS POST to the labeler's `report` URL of `{"subject": <subject>, "value": <one of the nine, or omitted>, "note": <up to 500 characters of the user's own words>}`. It MUST NOT carry anything else about the user: no browsing history, no other subjects, no identity, unless the user chooses to authenticate under [BRC-103](../peer-to-peer/0103.md) so the labeler can follow up.

6.5.4 **A report is evidence, never a label.** A host MUST NOT show a report as a label, count reports, or act on them. Nostr's experience is that reports are easily gamed and rarely read; a report reaches a user's screen only through a labeler that has examined it and put its name and evidence to the result.

### 7. The labeler's policy

7.1 `policy` is a public HTTPS document. It MUST state:

1. Which values the labeler applies, including any namespaced values, and the evidence it requires for each.
2. How quickly it acts on a report, and how it reviews its existing labels.
3. How a subject, or anybody, asks for a label to be corrected or removed, and an **appeal** route by which a decision is reconsidered by somebody not involved in making it, or a statement that no such person is available.
4. Whether, and how, it notifies a subject that it has been labeled. A labeler SHOULD notify an application's operator at its `metanet.app.support` URL when it applies `compromised`, because that operator is the party most able to act.
5. Any relationship it has with a subject it labels, including being its competitor, its curator, or its host.
6. Who holds its root and signer keys, in what number, and whether a person reviews labels before publication.
7. A dated history of changes to the policy.

7.2 A labeler MUST NOT apply a threat value to a subject on the ground of competition, commercial dispute, or the content of lawful speech. Automated labelers are no exception: Nostr's `tagr` bot, consumed by more than one client, labeled speech its operators disagreed with.

7.2.1 A labeler that labels automatically MUST say so in its policy, and SHOULD have a person review every threat label before it is published.

7.3 A labeler SHOULD separate the ability to propose a label from the ability to publish a feed, for the reason [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.9.3 gives for catalogs.

### 8. Extension

8.1 The nine core values and five subject kinds are closed, and change only by revision of this document.

8.2 A labeler that needs a value the core set does not provide publishes it under its own namespace, under section 3.3.

### 9. Not Specified Here

9.1 **How a labeler decides.** Detection, scanning, reports and investigation are the labeler's, and its policy says what they are.

9.2 **Labels about people, handles and content.** Labels here are about applications, sites, app keys and catalogs. Labels about [BRC-169](../peer-to-peer/0169.md) handles, or about content, raise questions of speech and reputation this document does not answer.

9.3 **Payment for labeling.** Whether a labeler charges anybody for anything is not specified, and section 7.1 item 5 requires any relationship with a labeled subject to be disclosed.

9.4 **Blocking.** A host or an organisation that wants to prevent access to labeled sites, for example on managed devices, is applying its own policy, and MUST NOT present that policy as a label or as this document's requirement.

### 10. Relationships

10.1 [BRC-241](./0241.md) supplies the identities, keys, chain and distribution this document reuses, with kind `labels` in field 3 of its outputs. Its section 7.8 is where labels meet catalogs, and its section 7.9.3 the single warning surface.

10.2 [BRC-116](../wallet/0116.md) is unchanged. Section 6.1.7 places a threat label in its connection prompt, and nothing a label says alters what the prompt grants.

10.3 [BRC-184](../wallet/0184.md)'s stewardship and dispute practice is adopted for labelers in section 7.

10.4 BRC-244 defines the build manifest and aggregate hash that a `build` subject names. A host that does not implement it ignores build labels.

### 11. Conformance

| Condition | Required behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Labeler publishes | Feeds declared in `metanet.labelers`, each a chain of kind `labels`; a version step when labels change; `policy` present |
| Host follows a feed | As a BRC-241 chain, from any source; refreshed at least daily; out of date after 7 days without a successful refresh, never for being quiet |
| New threat label or retraction | Effective once mined; waits on nothing else |
| Threat label | `ref` to public evidence, or ignored; shown before pin, first open, connection or catalog add, on the single warning surface, attributed; never blocks; never scored |
| Feed out of date | Threat labels still shown, dated, passive after 90 days; disclosures not shown as current |
| Disclosure or namespaced value | Detail view only, attributed, never interrupting |
| Dismissal | Per labeler, subject and value; passive display kept; interrupts again only on a new value, `ref` or labeler |
| `domainSuffix` at or above a public suffix | Ignored |
| Build label | Applied only by a host implementing BRC-244 that checked that build |
| Subscription | Two-way declaration first; 5 to 10 labelers, each covering up to eight feeds; one start subscribed once; name collisions said; any removable except one disclosed baseline |
| Moved app | Key labels follow; domain labels stay, shown passively |
| Labels obtained | Whole feeds, never per-subject queries |
| Retraction of a label the user saw | User told once |
| Report | Only to a labeler the user picked; only subject, value and note; never shown or counted as a label |
| No labels apply | Never "safe" |

### 12. Surfaces that fail conformance

1. A green "Safe" badge on a site no labeler has flagged.
2. "This site is a scam", in the wallet's voice, with no labeler named.
3. A threat warning that cannot be passed.
4. A connection prompt for a labeled site that shows nothing, because the site was not reached through a catalog.
5. "Risk score 87/100", or "Reported by 14 users".
6. A wallet that asks a labeler about each site the user visits.
7. A drainer's threat label silently dropped because the feed could not be refreshed overnight.
8. `custodial` shown in red beside the word "Warning", or a namespaced value in a red banner.
9. A label on `github.io` that flags every site hosted there.
10. A dismissed warning that reappears every morning because the labeler re-dated it.
11. A labeler's feed marked out of date because the labeler has not changed its labels in a month.
12. A report button that sends the user's recent sites along with the one reported.

## What Is Checkable, and What Is Taken on Trust

| Fact | Who can check it | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A label is the labeler's | Anybody | The chain from the start confirmed at subscription, and the document hash |
| What a labeler said, and when it retracted it | Anybody | The chain's version outputs, and the documents their hashes resolve to under UHRP |
| A labeler showed every user the same feed | Anybody | Each version spends the one before |
| A threat label has evidence | Anybody | Its `ref` |
| A host did not query per subject | A reviewer | Network traffic |

| Claim | Why it cannot be checked | What stands in for a check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A label is correct | It is a judgement | Its evidence, its labeler's name, an appeal, and the user's freedom to unsubscribe |
| A labeler is impartial | Motive is not observable | Section 7.1 item 5's disclosure, and section 7.2 |
| An unlabeled subject is harmless | Absence of a label is absence of a finding | Nothing; section 6.4.2 forbids implying otherwise |
| The feed a host holds is the newest | An unspent output cannot be proved unspent | Two or more sources asked at least daily, and out of date after 7 days without a successful refresh |

## What It Buys, and What It Costs

| Party | What they get | What it costs them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| User | Warnings from several named parties, at the moment of decision, for listed and unlisted sites alike, without anyone seeing their browsing | One interruption per threat, and choosing whom to listen to |
| Labeler | A channel to every conforming wallet without a commercial relationship with any, that survives its own servers and needs no reissuing to stay current | A funded wallet and a fraction of a cent per version, evidence for every threat label, a policy, an appeal route, and its name on every claim |
| App operator | A known party to answer to when labeled, a public record to contest, and notice when compromised | Exposure to labelers' judgement |
| Host | Threat intelligence from parties it need not vet or pay | Feed handling, and presenting other parties' claims honestly in its own interface |

**A labeler can hurt an honest application.** Section 7 makes that harm attributable, evidenced, appealable and public, and every user can unsubscribe from the labeler that caused it.

## Security Considerations

**A labeler is an attacker's target and an attacker's tool.** A compromised labeler key could label a legitimate application `malicious` across every subscribed wallet. Offline root keys that appoint replaceable signer keys under [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 6.6.4, and a chain on which every version stays on record, bound and expose this; section 5.1.4 limits how long a label from a feed nobody can refresh, or one whose chain has ended, keeps interrupting; and because labels never block, the attack degrades a reputation rather than a service.

**Suppression is the more dangerous attack.** Freezing a feed before a drainer is labeled gains more than forging a label. A withheld step is seen as soon as any source shows it, a host cut off for 7 days knows it (section 5.1.2), existing threat labels survive staleness (section 5.1.3), and nothing but mining delays a new label (section 5.1.6).

**Labels can be laundered through keys.** An attacker who rotates app keys and domains together escapes key and domain labels both. Build labels under BRC-244, `domainSuffix` labels and the impersonation check of [BRC-241](./0241.md) section 7.5 are the remaining defences, and none is complete: a build label follows only code that has not been changed by a single byte.

**False negatives are the normal case.** Most malicious sites claim victims before any labeler flags them, and the wallet's BRC-116 prompt is then the only defence.

## Privacy Considerations

**Feeds are downloaded whole**, under section 5.3, so a labeler learns that a host exists and nothing about what its user visits. A host that fetches feeds from mirrors or UHRP hosts tells the labeler less still.

**Chain lookups reveal subscriptions.** A host refreshing a feed asks overlay hosts by its start, which tells them which labelers its user subscribes to. Hosts SHOULD batch these lookups with the refresh schedule.

**Subscriptions are a profile.** The set of labelers a user trusts says something about them. Hosts MUST NOT disclose it to any labeler, curator or application.

**Labels name subjects publicly.** Evidence (section 4.2.5), appeal (section 7), retraction notice (section 4.4) and notice of a `compromised` label (section 7.1 item 4) are the operator's protections.

## Implementations

No host implements this document.

**Existing threat lists** map onto it directly. MetaMask's `eth-phishing-detect` blocklist, published in the same form, would be a feed of `phishing` domain labels with the list's own entries as evidence. A WalletConnect Verify `isScam` result is a threat label from a single, fixed labeler. Neither carries per-label evidence today, which section 4.2.5 would require.

**Bluesky's Ozone** models the reviewer workflow a labeler under section 7 needs.

**BSV Radar** already records rejection reasons and defunct apps, which correspond to `malicious`, `impersonation` and `unmaintained`, and could publish them as a feed.

**A baseline.** The BSV Association, or a security organisation it funds at arm's length, is the obvious candidate for a baseline labeler in Harbor and its forks. It should be separate from the BSV Association's own catalog, so that the party that selects applications is not the one warning against their competitors.

### Conformance levels

**Labeler.** Declares each feed with its start; publishes each as a chain of kind `labels`, with a version step whenever its labels change and evidence for every threat label; publishes its documents under UHRP where it can; retracts in public; and maintains a policy with an appeal route.

**Host.** Meets every row of section 11's table.

**Verification.** Chains are checked as BRC-241 catalogs are; behaviour against section 11's table, most simply with a feed labeling one listed app, one unlisted site, one app key and one catalog; a step one source withholds; a feed unchanged for a month; a host cut off for eight days; and a dismissed label re-dated.

## Failure Modes

| What went wrong | How it shows | Detected by | Attributable to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drainer not yet labeled | Nothing | The victim | Nobody; labels are additive. The wallet's prompt is the remaining defence |
| Labeler flagged a competitor | Users warned about an honest app | The operator, through the evidence and appeal | The labeler, by name |
| Sources withhold the newest step | Seen as soon as one source shows it; otherwise out of date after 7 days, threats still shown | The host | The sources |
| Labeler's feed ended | Threat labels dated, then passive after 90 days | The host | The labeler |
| Signer key stolen | False labels, permanently on chain, until a root step appoints new signers | Anybody following the chain | The labeler, which takes a root step and retracts |
| Over-broad suffix label | Many sites flagged | Users and operators | The labeler; public suffixes are refused outright |
| User dismissed a correct warning | Funds lost | The user | Nobody but the attacker. The choice was the user's, and was informed |

## Appendix A: Worked Example

Illustrative. Domains are invented; no key, transaction or hash is printed because none has been made.

A Harbor user has a baseline labeler, `labels:security.example/threats`, and at a meetup scans a QR code for `labels:collectors.example/feeds`. The wallet fetches `collectors.example`'s manifest, finds two declared feeds, confirms and pins both starts, follows each chain from two overlay hosts, and shows one surface: the labeler's domain and name, the values it applies, including `p collectors.example counterfeit`, how many labels of each it holds, and its policy. One subscription covers both feeds.

**A warning.** The user opens a link to `readlngs.example` from a chat. The BRC-116 connection prompt shows, on the one warning surface: "security.example says this site impersonates another app (4 Nov 2026). Evidence." The user declines; had they connected anyway, nothing would have stopped them.

**A dismissal.** The user dismisses a `phishing` label on their employer's staging site. The next morning `security.example` re-dates the label; nothing interrupts. A week later it changes the label's `ref` to a new case, and the user is interrupted once more.

**The labeler's server goes down.** `security.example` is offline for two days. The wallet's daily refresh finds a new version step through one of two overlay hosts, fetches the document from a UHRP host by its hash, and shows the new `phishing` label it carries at once.
