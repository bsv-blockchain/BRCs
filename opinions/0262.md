# BRC-262: What Bitcoin Owes the Person Who Wasn’t There

> Ty Everett (ty@projectbabbage.com)

In November 2008, Hal Finney asked Satoshi Nakamoto whether honest participants could tell if an attacker used superior computing power to erase a payment. Satoshi’s answer turned on the person who wasn’t there. A merchant might remember seeing her payment confirmed before she shipped the goods. A dozen witnesses might remember it too. But someone joining the network the next morning could inspect two competing histories without being able to establish, from either alone, which one the merchant had seen first. “The CPU power proof-of-work vote must have the final say,” Satoshi wrote on November 8. He was not describing an innocent tie between miners. He meant the rule to hold even when the attacker won. ([metzdowd.com](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2008-November/014832.html))

That rule gives the newcomer an answer that does not depend on whom she knows. The merchant, however, has a different question. A buyer can sign two conflicting payments of the same coins, one to her and one back to himself. If each appears on a different branch, neither branch spends the coins twice within its own history. An honest node can therefore follow the rules and accept the branch of a dishonest payer. Satoshi’s white paper recognizes both sides of this bargain: it describes deliberately taking back a payment as fraud, and it explains why the risk of a successful reversal falls as confirmations accumulate, provided honest miners control most of the computing power. In his solution to the Byzantine generals’ problem, honesty is an assumption about how participants behave. It is not a distinction awarded to whoever wins. ([bitcoin.org](https://www.bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf))

What, then, did Bitcoin’s first users agree to follow? Satoshi’s release announcement of January 8, 2009, placed two claims side by side. Total circulation would be 21 million coins. The software was experimental, and its state might have to be restarted. A warning about an alpha release is slender authority for overturning a mature ledger. But the distinction would matter sooner than anyone might have wished: Satoshi had made a commitment about the money without promising to preserve every outcome of the program he had written. ([metzdowd.com](https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2009-January/014994.html))

On July 17, 2010, Satoshi released software with a *checkpoint*. An updated node would insist on retaining a particular block, about 200 blocks in the past, even if a rival history later accumulated more work. There was only one known version of that block when he chose it. He was protecting a settled past, not asking software to rule on an allegation of fraud in a live dispute. Still, his parenthesis was consequential: the safeguard applied “if you have this update.” Once installed, the checkpoint was an objective test. Installing it was a choice. In August, Satoshi expressly identified his manual checkpoints as the exception to otherwise unrestricted reorganization. ([bitcointalk.org](https://bitcointalk.org/index.php?topic=437.0))

Two defects that summer made the case for correction unusually clear. July’s OP_RETURN scripting flaw, CVE-2010-5141, could have allowed someone to spend another person’s coins without authorization. It was fixed without known exploitation on the main network. ([raw.githubusercontent.com](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/CVEProject/cvelistV5/main/cves/2010/5xxx/CVE-2010-5141.json)) On August 15, a separate arithmetic error, CVE-2010-5139, allowed a transaction in block 74,638 to create more than 184 billion bitcoins. Satoshi released a patch that day. On August 16 he reported that miners building on the repaired branch had overtaken the defective one. Here the newcomer needed no opinion about the character of the person who submitted the transaction. She could read the issuance promise and do the arithmetic. ([raw.githubusercontent.com](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/CVEProject/cvelistV5/main/cves/2010/5xxx/CVE-2010-5139.json))

After the overflow, Satoshi built a way to reach people before they acted on bad information. His August 27 release could display a signed warning and temporarily suspend certain automated wallet functions. An alert could not stop mining or designate the winning branch. Nor did Satoshi claim permanence for the arrangement: that December he removed the power of a signed alert to trigger the suspension, while retaining the warning channel. Bitcoin Core retired the channel itself years later, citing the risks of concentrating influence in one key. The original design choice is instructive precisely because of its limits. The key could establish who had sent a warning. It could not establish that the warning was right. ([satoshi.nakamotoinstitute.org](https://satoshi.nakamotoinstitute.org/posts/bitcointalk/threads/206/))

In March 2013, a newer version of Bitcoin accepted a block that some older nodes could not process. About 60 percent of mining power was supporting the newer branch. Two mining pools, BTC Guild and Slush, switched back to the older software, sacrificing revenue to restore a history both groups could accept. The greater work visible afterward was partly the *consequence* of that decision. It could not have been the reason the miners made it. Nor did their coordination make mining optional. To reunite the network, they still had to do the work. ([github.com](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0050.mediawiki))

The harder case came in 2021 on Bitcoin SV, or BSV, a separate network from BTC. In July, Bitcoin Association reported that a miner had withheld blocks and released competing histories. BitMart, an exchange, said it had been deceived into crediting accounts with 91,000 BSV. The order in *GBM Global Holding Company Limited v. The Unidentified Individuals Listed on Schedule A*, No. 21-cv-06284 (S.D.N.Y. July 13, 2022), confirmed a $5,231,549.42 arbitration award. The arbitrator had received uncontested affidavits and documentary evidence; the judge reviewed the award deferentially, not by conducting a contested trial of the underlying fraud. That record gives substance to the claim of harm in July. It does not adjudicate what happened in August. ([coingeek.com](https://coingeek.com/faqs-july-2021-block-withholding-re-organisation-attack-on-the-bsv-network/))

In early August 2021, Lucas Nuzzi and his colleagues at Coin Metrics observed a reorganization that displaced 14 blocks. Their report documented the reorganization, not a financial loss at an exchange in that incident. ([1ucas.me](https://1ucas.me/research/identifying-the-bsv-51-attack)) Bitcoin Association believed the July attacker had returned. In its August 4 update, it published three block hashes and recommended that operators use `invalidateblock` to reject them. An operator who complied would exclude those blocks and their descendants regardless of their accumulated work. This was a coordinated exception to automatic chain selection. The hashes tell the newcomer exactly *what* she was being asked to reject. They do not, by themselves, tell her *why*. ([coingeek.com](https://coingeek.com/statement-on-august-2021-block-re-organisation-attack-on-the-bitcoin-sv-network/))

Who had the authority to make that choice? An operator could decide what its own node would accept. A miner could decide where to put its work. A merchant could decide which ledger to recognize in payment. No one of them could make a history common by declaration. That required others to converge on it; if two groups kept mining and using incompatible histories, the result would be a fork. Convergence explains how a decision takes effect. It does not prove that the decision was just. ([bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/en/bitcoin-core/2016-01-07-statement?utm_source=openai))

The cost to the newcomer is real. Given both histories, she can check the signatures, the spending conditions, the conflicting transactions and the work. She cannot tell from the blocks alone whether one was deliberately withheld or merely delayed, whether BitMart credited an account, or whether a parcel left a warehouse. Satoshi’s white paper envisioned a warning that would prompt a lightweight client to fetch a disputed block and check an inconsistency for itself. No fuller download will produce a shipping receipt. An exceptional exclusion cannot offer the same kind of verification as ordinary consensus. Its defenders owe the newcomer the evidence they relied on, the inferences they drew, and an account of what remains uncertain. Putting their decision into code makes the result reproducible. It does not turn testimony into cryptographic proof. ([bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf))

That difference calls for two different thresholds. A credible warning can justify alerting other operators, preserving records, delaying credit or suspending deposits. Indeed, in July the association itself urged exchanges to monitor reorganizations and increase confirmation requirements. An exchange need not prove a fraud before deciding to wait longer for its next deposit. But asking other people to set aside a greater-work history means asking them to revisit payments *they have already accepted*. Even during the clear-cut repair of 2010, a user called Ground Loop had to ask what would become of an ordinary payment he had sent on the discarded branch. Satoshi expected the payment to be included again, but its confirmations would start over. Someone who had already waited would have to wait again. ([coingeek.com](https://coingeek.com/faqs-july-2021-block-withholding-re-organisation-attack-on-the-bsv-network/))

The higher threshold is necessity and proportionality in defense of predictable settlement. A seller who takes payment and never ships has cheated the buyer, but the ledger has recorded their transaction faithfully. A campaign that reliably reverses confirmed payments threatens the means of settlement itself. Even then, proponents of exclusion must connect the *particular branch* to that threat, explain why a less disruptive response would not suffice, and show how excluding it would allow ordinary settlement to resume. They must count the unrelated payments put at risk and the danger of prolonging the split. The rule must be one they would accept before knowing who stands to lose. It must also pass a merchant’s test: **if this rule were applied every time, would she have a better basis for deciding when to ship?** On the public record, the 2021 incidents warranted coordinated precautions. The August notice alone did not establish that each of its three exclusions met that higher burden. Where the burden is not met, the ordinary greatest-work rule should stand. ([1ucas.me](https://1ucas.me/research/identifying-the-bsv-51-attack))

The distinction became concrete on February 15, 2024, when BSV Association published Network Access Rules assigning itself a formal role in issuing directives, including, under specified conditions, instructions to invalidate blocks. Publication establishes what authority the association claimed, not that every participant accepted it or that it existed in 2021. An operator can heed an adviser on the merits of one warning without appointing that adviser to decide the next dispute. An emergency may require judgment. It need not create a judge. ([nar.bsvblockchain.org](https://nar.bsvblockchain.org/))

Proof of work can resolve a contest among histories only after participants have established which histories their rules permit them to consider. Accumulating more work cannot settle whether those rules were rightly chosen, any more than it can settle whether an emergency departure from them was legitimate. For those judgments the newcomer may have to rely on records that other people preserved. That is different from having to submit to authority those people continue to exercise. She can examine an archive, challenge a witness and disagree with a conclusion. None of that requires giving the keeper of the archive a vote over her next payment. ([w.econinfosec.org](https://w.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2013/papers/KrollDaveyFeltenWEIS2013.pdf))

Bitcoin’s capacity for collective correction was present from the beginning. A standing right to pronounce otherwise valid histories dishonest was not. Refusing a greater-work branch can, in exceptional circumstances, preserve the purpose of Satoshi’s design without being an instruction of its original chain-selection rule. Those asking others to make that exception must show that they are protecting the conditions under which strangers can settle their affairs, not merely protecting a favored party from a loss. They owe the person who wasn’t there an explanation she can examine—and a network she can use the next morning without having to ask their permission.
